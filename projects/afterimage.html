afterimage: a cyanotype game about seeing the same thing differently

afterimage is a photogram camera game. There’s no lens: players arrange patterned filters directly on light-sensitive cyanotype paper, expose it to UV light, and wash it, and only then see the print they made. Each round starts with a card. Sometimes you try to recreate a reference image as closely as you can; sometimes you get a single word and interpret it however you like. Two people can start with the same prompt and the same filters and still end up with completely different prints, and afterimage makes that difference visible. Natalie Tan, Shelly Yang, and I designed and built it as an analog camera project for MIT 4.031 Objects and Interaction.

01 · Design question

What were we trying to make?

Creating art is often an individual experience. What if making an image together, and revealing it together, could start a conversation about how differently two people see? “You start with the same tools. The interesting part is seeing where you take them.” From our rulebook.

Cyanotypes are made with light, shadow, and a little bit of unpredictability. You arrange things on the paper, expose it, wash it, and only then find out how it turned out. We wanted to turn that process into a game. Two people can look at the same image and notice completely different things, or hear the same word and picture completely different scenes. afterimage makes those differences visible: recreating an image tests how closely you can observe and rebuild it, and interpreting a word shows how differently two people translate the same idea.

02 · How it works

Same tools, two prints

Two cyanotype workstations sit side by side. Each player gets a fresh sheet of cyanotype paper and an identical set of ten pattern filters, black ink printed on clear film. Black blocks the UV light and the clear film lets it through, so wherever a filter’s pattern sits, the paper stays pale, and everywhere else it develops into deep blue.

  1. Draw a card. A reference card shows an image made with the same filters you have; a theme card shows a single word.
  2. Compose. Rotate, overlap, and stack filters on the paper, place them partly off the page, or leave parts of it empty. Small changes in position or spacing can completely change the final image.
  3. Expose. Put the composition under UV light. Too little exposure gives a faint print; too much can make some of the contrast disappear. You learn to judge it, but there is always some uncertainty.
  4. Wash and reveal. Washing brings out the print: the blue deepens and the shapes sharpen. It may or may not match what you imagined. Either way, the reveal is part of the game.
  • Mode 1

    Recreate

    Same image. Same tools. Who gets closer?

    Both players work out how a reference card was made (which shapes overlap, which are rotated, where the light and dark areas fall) and build their own version. The print closest to the reference wins the round and keeps the card.

  • Mode 2

    Interpret

    Same prompt. Completely different ideas.

    Both players respond to the same theme word, like balance, which could become a symmetrical composition or two shapes that seem to hold each other up. There’s no right answer; you reveal the prints together and talk about why you made your choices.

  • Mode 3

    Solo

    Keep experimenting.

    Recreate a reference card and note what you got right, what shifted, and what you’d change, then try the same card again. Or draw a theme card and make whatever comes to mind, with no score at all.

03 · The object

One box, two workstations

Everything packs into a single hinged box, 12.5 by 5 inches. Inside, a divided middle caddy holds the cards and blank cyanotype paper, and it’s flanked by two camera boxes, one per player. Each camera box is roughly a 4-inch cube (4.25 × 4.125 × 4.125 inches) with an open 0.75-inch tray on top and ten stacked slots below that hold the filter frames. The frames’ pull tabs step diagonally so you can grab any filter on its own, and the tabs are marked with filter codes, like F06 below.

04 · The prints

Ten filters, 112 cards

The game runs on a small visual system. We designed ten filters (fine pinstripes, offset pinstripes, an almost-horizontal set, echo rings, growing dots, ripple lines, chevron folds, a broken checker, radial spokes, and diagonal dashes), each a 4-inch square printed on transparency film. From those we generated every reference image a player could be asked to recreate: all ten singles and all 45 two-filter pairings, plus twelve optical variants that overlap filters at small rotations (up to 20 degrees) for moiré, interference, and tilt illusions, and twelve timed exposures.

In the timed cards you switch the arrangement halfway through the exposure (conceptually five minutes, then five more). A filter that sits on the paper for only one half blocks the light for half the time, so its pattern prints as a lighter blue, while anything covered for the whole exposure stays white. Whatever you take away or add partway leaves a pale trace in the final print, a kind of afterimage.

Those images became a 112-card deck: 55 reference cards for the singles and pairings, 12 timed, 12 optical, and 33 theme words, from balance and echo to threshold and afterimage itself. Reference cards carry a point value, from 1 for a single filter up to 5 for a timed exposure.

05 · Process

What we actually did

  1. Sketching two kinds of camera

    We sketched a lens version, with a lens on the front of the box, and a photogram version, where the image is made by laying things directly on the paper. Around them we tried ways to start a round: dice, a spinning wheel of symbols, a card shuffler that ended up looking a lot like a slot machine. The photogram version won, and cards beat the dice and the wheel.

  2. Working out the pieces

    More sketches sized the filter frames, the stacked filter storage, and tabs on the filters for easy removal, which made it into the final camera boxes. A low-fidelity mockup in black board showed how a lidded box, a divided caddy, and two separate camera boxes could nest together.

  3. Drawing and rendering it

    We drew every part with dimensions (the outer box, the lid, the middle caddy, and the camera box with its ten slots at 0.325-inch pitch) and checked the fit: 4.25 + 3.25 + 4.25 inches of inner boxes inside a 12-inch interior. A Blender exploded render showed how it all comes apart.

  4. Designing the filters and cards

    We designed the ten filters, generated every single and two-filter combination, and added the optical and timed variants. The deck went through rounds of edits: removing point badges from the theme cards, cutting instructional hint text from the card fronts, and reordering the deck.

  5. Building it

    The boxes are wood with 45-degree mitered corners. How you cut and assembled them, and how the filter frames and the lid graphics were made.

06 · Lessons learned

What we’d tell ourselves at the start

  • Sometimes, less is more

    At first we had too many components and complicated rules, which made the game difficult to explain.

  • Clarity is a good indicator of decent design

    If the game can be explained in a short summary without confusing players, that’s a strong sign the design is intuitive. If not, there might be too much going on.

  • Ambition vs. feasibility

    Prioritize the essential features that make the game engaging, intuitive, and enjoyable, rather than adding complexity that doesn’t meaningfully improve how it plays.

  • Not with a bandsaw

    Do not use a bandsaw to cut 45-degree angles for corner joints (rip Shelly).
