Iterative User-Centered Evaluation of a Machine Learning–Augmented Postoperative Risk Summary Report

Machine learning models can flag surgical patients at risk for complications like delirium, pneumonia, or blood clots, but only if clinicians can read the prediction, trust it, and act on it at the right moment. This past summer, with the SAFE-T Lab, led by Dr. Joanna Abraham, at the Washington University in St. Louis School of Medicine, I worked on two studies of tools used during OR to ICU handoffs. For the first, I observed handoffs from inside operating rooms as surgeries ended and patients were transferred to the ICU team, since that handoff is where the risk report would eventually live. For the second, I helped conduct the third phase of a usability study of an ML-augmented risk report for postoperative ICU care.

My role
Bioinformatics Research Intern, BIDS program. I observed OR to ICU handoffs, coded the observation notes into themes, helped run the phase III cognitive walkthroughs, and was first author on the summer poster.

01 · Research question

What were we trying to find out?

Is an ML-augmented postoperative risk report usable, interpretable, and feasible within real ICU workflows, and how should it change from one iteration to the next? Adapted from the study goal on our poster.
02 · Context

Why this mattered

Postoperative complications are common, and they hit hardest for high-risk surgical patients who go to the ICU. Machine learning models can identify who is at risk early enough to prevent some of those complications. The team’s earlier work used a user-centered design approach to understand postoperative care challenges, build risk prediction models, and co-design a prototype risk report.

A prediction only helps if it reaches the right clinician at the right moment. One of those moments is the handoff, when a patient leaves the operating room for the ICU and everything that happened in surgery, what’s running, and what to watch for next has to travel with them. So the work had two halves, which were to understand the handoff as it happens today and to test whether clinicians can actually use the risk report.

03 · Methods

How we approached it, and why

The evaluation was an iterative, convergent mixed-methods usability study (mixed-methods meaning it combined observation and interviews with questionnaires) across three phases, with OR and ICU clinicians at WashU Medicine reviewing real surgery cases headed to the ICU. Each session ran 1.5–2 hours. I did the handoff observations and the open coding myself, helped conduct the phase III cognitive walkthroughs, and coded the clinicians’ feedback from them into themes.

04 · Process

What I actually did

  1. Watching the handoff

    Late May–early July 2026

    For a quality improvement study of the Epic-embedded postoperative handoff tool, I found myself decked out head to toe in blue scrubs, watching from the edge of operating rooms as surgeries ended. I was there to observe the current workflow and to encourage the anesthesia team to use the tool, and I took timestamped notes as the team prepared the patient, transported them, and handed off to the ICU team. When ICU clinicians had a moment, I asked for their feedback on the tool, and I also designed flyers to raise awareness of it. Something that happened a lot was someone from the care team jotting down notes on a scrap of paper, a wrapper, or the back of a napkin, perhaps because those were already nearby and the handoff report was only accessible on the phone through several clicks. Every team had its own way of passing information along, and it seemed to depend as much on who was standing where and what was within reach as on the tools themselves.

  2. Making sense of the notes

    June 2026

    I transcribed handwritten notes into a spreadsheet and coded every sentence for statements about the report, and for barriers and facilitators in the handoff workflow. Patterns across the codes became three themes, which were perceived usefulness of the report, challenges (with the workflow, the tool, and integrating it), and suggestions for improvement and implementation.

  3. Presenting it as a website instead of slides

    July 2026

    For the mid-program presentation I built the site above, so people could trace a patient’s journey from the OR to the ICU and open the proposed additions to the workflow themselves.

  4. Evaluating the ML risk report

    July 2026

    In the third phase of the usability study, clinicians worked through handoff tasks with a more advanced version of the risk report while we observed and recorded. The main objective of the walkthroughs was to identify any remaining usability issues, and I took notes on everything (I’m talking everything they said, their non-verbal cues, etc.). Afterward, I coded the clinicians’ feedback into themes.

  5. Pulling three phases together

    Late July 2026

    We analyzed all three phases together and summarized the results in a poster at the end of the program, which I put together as first author.

05 · Findings

What we learned

  • Clinicians saw real value in the report

    They described using it to anticipate complications, to prepare for handoffs and get ahead on notes, and as a teaching tool.

  • Interpretation was the biggest barrier

    Risk categories without percentages or thresholds were hard to read, Shapley values (SHAP) were unfamiliar, since clinicians simply didn’t know what they meant, and features that didn’t seem clinically relevant lowered trust in the model.

  • The visuals sometimes contradicted the data

    Red bars on a patient labeled low risk, bar lengths that didn’t match their values, and color coding that disagreed with clinicians’ own judgment all caused confusion.

  • Clinicians worried about what the score would mean in practice

    Clinicians worried that associations would be read as causes, about legal accountability when they disagree with the model, and about what they’re actually supposed to do with a risk score.

Usability improved across the iterations, with overall PSSUQ scores going from 3.25 in Phase I to 3.20 in Phase II and 2.83 in Phase III, where lower is better. Usability had improved, but clinicians were still hesitant to trust the report, mostly because of the way information was being communicated to them. Consistent with prior work, explainability and interpretability remained the key barriers to clinician trust and adoption.

06 · Outcome

What came out of it

I presented a poster on the study at the end of the BIDS summer program. To inform implementation, the team observed one hundred postoperative handoffs and mapped the existing workflow to find where the report could fit, and we proposed integrating the ML risk report into the Epic-embedded handoff tool through a SMART on FHIR link.

The next step for the team is to refine the report based on the latest findings, integrate it into the handoff tool, and evaluate its effect on care continuity, clinician adoption, and patient safety. The report had already gone through more than a year of iteration before I joined its third phase, and finding out whether it changes how clinicians make decisions will take longer still. Since then, I’ve stayed on with the SAFE-T Lab for a scoping review of EHR-derived indicators of care team teamwork.

07 · Reflection

What worked, what didn’t, what’s next

What worked

Being in the operating room before evaluating the report. Watching how people were actually using the technology we create for them, down to the notes on napkins, showed me that the real problems with trust only show up once a tool is in front of the people who have to use it.

Limitations

Thirteen clinicians across nineteen sessions, all at one health system. Trust is also harder to measure than usability. In the walkthroughs, I could hear that the clinicians were hesitant, but I had no way to measure whether they would follow the report when it was right and question it when it was wrong.

What I’d study next

That left me asking which forms of explanation fit how a clinician already thinks, and whether that fit helps clinicians tell when the model is right and when it isn’t, instead of just making them trust it more. The same risk estimate has to make sense to a clinician in the OR, a clinician in the ICU, and potentially the patient, and each of them thinks about risk differently, so I want to build alternatives to the Shapley values framed the way each reader already talks about risk and test which form fits which reader.
