Framing Financial Feedback: Narrative vs. Factual AI Explanation in an Educational Simulation

The “What-If” Financial Decision-Making Simulation is a serious game that uses explainable AI to help college students build financial literacy. Players work through budgeting, saving, and borrowing scenarios, and after each choice a custom GPT-4 model, one per condition, explains what happened and why in either a narrative or a factual mode. It began as a group research project in MAS 366 Human–AI Interaction at Wellesley College in Fall 2025, and that version was accepted to ACM SIGDOC 2026. In Spring 2026 I continued the work with Professor Orit Shaer, my PI, as an independent study, taking it in a different direction.

01 · Research question

What were we trying to find out?

How does narrative versus factual framing in AI-generated feedback affect how college students comprehend and internalize financial literacy concepts? And does the form an explanation takes change how people feel about the content, separately from what they learn from it?
02 · Context

Why this mattered

Traditional financial literacy programs teach abstract concepts with one curriculum for everyone, so students who don’t already know much about money have a hard time connecting what they learn to everyday decisions like budgeting, saving, and whether to spend or invest.

Serious games can help by letting people try out consequences without any real-world risk, and AI can give immediate feedback on each choice. However, the form that feedback takes is a communication design decision in itself. The same outcome can be explained in a narrative way or as a set of numbers, and prior work on explainable AI shows that narrative framing can improve comprehension and trust. What hadn’t been studied was how those explanation styles work in a serious game for learners who aren’t experts.

03 · Methods

How we approached it, and why

To find out whether one explanation mode works better than another, we needed to compare them under controlled conditions, so we held everything constant except the explanation style. Both versions of the game deliver the same scenarios, the same consequences, and the same underlying financial principles, and only the surface language changes.

05 · Findings

What we learned

  • The narrative group more often called the concepts common sense

    In the debrief, participants in the factual condition were less likely to describe the financial concepts as “common sense,” so feedback given as balances and interest calculations may make the same concept feel less like something you already knew, even the first time you see it.

  • Phrasing changed how the concepts felt rather than what people learned

    Both conditions got identical information, so the way the feedback was phrased is the most likely reason for the difference. We think it changes what people count as new knowledge and what they feel they already knew.

  • Delayed gratification came up everywhere

    Across both conditions, the mid-study surveys brought up budgeting and prioritization, debt avoidance, and how interest rates work, and delayed gratification came up at all three levels, so the structure of the simulation got people reflecting whichever mode they were in.

  • We found no significant difference in what the two groups learned

    Quiz scores didn’t differ significantly between the two conditions. With twenty participants from one selective college, a lot of them may have already known the material, so a ceiling effect could have hidden a short-term difference.

07 · Reflection

What worked, what didn’t, what’s next

What worked

One or two things you’d do the same way again.

Limitations

We only had a small sample (N = 20), all from a single private liberal arts college, where students may already have more exposure to personal finance, which could produce a ceiling effect.

What’s next

Since then, I’ve continued the work as an independent study, where Professor Shaer pushed me to rethink the question itself, and coming back with counterfactual explanations and measures of individual differences, like need for cognition and narrative transportation, changed what we were asking, which is what type of explanation suits which person. What’s still next from the class version is expanding the simulation into a web app with a live financial dashboard, a conversational AI advisor, and a what-if toggle, recruiting more broadly, and testing retention after a week.
